The Longer Story

A nonstop flight from Phoenix to London is remarkable precisely because modern travel has trained us to treat it as ordinary. In June 1919, John Alcock and Arthur Whitten Brown made the first nonstop transatlantic crossing in a modified Vickers Vimy — sixteen hours and twenty-seven minutes, landing nose-down in an Irish bog because the solid-looking ground wasn't. Heathrow itself opened in 1946 on the site of a wartime airfield, its first passenger terminals converted military tents along Bath Road. A little more than a century after Alcock and Brown, and eighty years after Heathrow's first postwar flight, our family leaves the Arizona desert and wakes up over England.

How Far North, Really?

It doesn't feel like it looking at a map, but this trip is a serious trip north. Omaha sits at about 41°N and Phoenix at about 33°N — squarely in the middle latitudes, on par with places like Beijing or Madrid. London, by contrast, sits at 51.5°N: roughly the same latitude as the Canada–US border running through Winnipeg, well north of every U.S. state except Alaska. Gullane and St Andrews, on Scotland's east coast, sit higher still at about 56°N — in North American terms, that's on par with the southern tip of Hudson Bay or the middle of Newfoundland.

What keeps the British Isles livable at that latitude, rather than sub-Arctic, is the North Atlantic Current — the same ocean conveyor belt that starts as the Gulf Stream off Florida. It carries warm water (and warm air with it) across the Atlantic and up along the U.K.'s coast, which is why London's September highs sit in the mid-60s°F, gentler than Omaha's often are, despite London sitting ten degrees of latitude farther from the equator. St Andrews and Gullane, being coastal and further north, trade a few of those degrees for wind and quicker-changing skies — golfers there have always built their game around it rather than waiting for it to pass. It's the reason a Scottish links course plays nothing like a course in Arizona, even though both are, technically, just grass and weather.

Eppley Airfield

Read the full story

The land was acquired by the City of Omaha in 1925 as an addition to Levi Carter Park, and pilots were using the field almost as soon as it was cleared — the earliest images show a grass strip with a single windsock. A 1934 terminal at what was then called the American Legion Municipal Airport eventually grew into today's Eppley Airfield, renamed for Eugene C. Eppley, the Omaha hotel-chain magnate whose estate funded the airport's 1959–60 jet-age upgrade after his death.

By April 1957, the airport was already handling more than 40 scheduled departures a day split between United Airlines and the long-gone Braniff International — respectable traffic for a mid-sized Midwestern city more than a decade before Eppley's terminal was rebuilt for the jet age. Southwest's gates on the B concourse today sit on ground that's been moving travelers for a full century, through prop planes, the jet age, deregulation, and everything since.

The airfield has had its share of rough days too — a military plane crashed near the field in a 1978 engine fire that killed everyone aboard, and in April 2024 a tornado tore through the property and damaged or destroyed roughly 30 small aircraft hangars, closing the airport only briefly before it reopened. Southwest, American, and Delta remain the three biggest carriers moving passengers through Eppley today.

Southwest Airlines

Read the full story

Southwest started in 1971 with three Boeing 737s flying triangle routes between Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio — a scrappy, no-frills challenger that spent its first years fighting off lawsuits from bigger Texas carriers just to get off the ground. It became the model for the modern low-cost carrier: single aircraft type (still all Boeing 737s today, over fifty years later), no assigned seats for most of its history, and a point-to-point network rather than the hub-and-spoke system everyone else used.

The strategy worked well enough that Southwest became one of the most consistently profitable airlines in U.S. history, posting profitable years for decades running before the pandemic finally broke that streak. Omaha to Phoenix is a routine short hop for the airline today — barely two hours — but it's still flying the same basic playbook that built the company.

Southwest is also a genuinely major presence in Phoenix specifically: by some measures it carries a slightly larger share of Sky Harbor's passengers than American Airlines does, despite Phoenix being only a "focus city" for Southwest rather than a true hub — a reflection of just how deeply the airline is woven into how the Southwest (the region) actually travels.

Phoenix Sky Harbor

Read the full story

Sky Harbor's story starts in 1928, when Scenic Airways owner J. Parker Van Zandt built what was at the time Phoenix's fourth airfield. Scenic didn't survive the 1929 stock market crash, and the airfield passed to the Acme Investment Company before the City of Phoenix bought it outright in 1935 for $100,000. Because of how isolated it felt at the time, locals simply called it "the farm."

Terminal 4 — the Barry M. Goldwater Terminal, named for the senator and Arizona native — broke ground in 1989 and opened in November 1990 at a cost of $248 million, the largest capital construction project in Phoenix history at the time. It opened with five concourses and 44 gates and immediately handled 70 percent of the airport's total traffic; today it handles the overwhelming majority of everything moving through Sky Harbor, American Airlines included.

The airport played an unplanned role in the growth of Phoenix itself: World War II training airfields and refueling stations brought thousands of young servicemen through the area, and many of them liked it enough to move their families back after the war — a genuine population boom the airport helped set in motion decades before anyone thought of Phoenix as a retirement or relocation destination. Sky Harbor sits close enough to downtown that you can see the skyline from the runway, a detail that makes today's family reunion feel like it's happening in the middle of the city rather than on its outskirts.

American Airlines & the 777-200ER

Read the full story

American Airlines traces its roots to a 1926 mail-carrying operation and became one of the "Big Four" U.S. carriers by the 1930s, eventually growing into the largest airline in the world by fleet size. The Boeing 777-200ER — the aircraft flying tonight's route — entered service in 1997 as one of the first twin-engine jets certified to fly long stretches of open ocean far from any diversion airport, a certification called ETOPS (Extended-range Twin-engine Operations).

That certification is precisely what makes a nonstop Phoenix–London routing possible on two engines instead of the four a similar-range aircraft would have needed a generation earlier — fewer engines to maintain and fuel, which is a large part of why long-haul flying got dramatically cheaper starting in the late 1990s. The 777-200ER remains one of the most-produced widebody aircraft ever built, still flying long routes for airlines worldwide nearly three decades after its debut.

The wider 777 family has quietly become one of the safest and most reliable aircraft programs in aviation history, and the "Triple Seven" nickname has stuck precisely because there's genuinely nothing else shaped quite like it — the largest twinjet ever built when it launched, and still instantly recognizable by its distinctive round fuselage and six-wheel main landing gear bogies.

Heathrow, eighty years on

Read the full story

Heathrow began as a small private airfield in the 1930s, was requisitioned for military use during the Second World War, and opened as London's civil airport in 1946 — its first passenger facilities were converted military tents along Bath Road, with travelers crossing wooden walkways over mud to reach their aircraft. It is genuinely difficult to overstate how improvised those early years were for what's now one of the busiest airports on Earth.

Terminal 3, where AA194 lands, opened in 1961 as the "Oceanic Terminal" — purpose-built for long-haul routes to the U.S. and Asia specifically, a signal of how important transatlantic traffic already was to Heathrow's future just fifteen years after it opened. The family lands eighty years after Heathrow's first postwar passengers did; the terminal is bigger, warmer, and considerably less muddy.

Heathrow has grown into the busiest airport in Europe and one of the busiest in the world by international passenger traffic, despite operating with only two runways — a genuine constraint most rival hub airports don't share, and one that's made a third runway one of the most politically contested infrastructure debates in modern British history, argued over for decades without resolution.

The Heathrow Express

Read the full story

The Heathrow Express opened in 1998 on a dedicated stretch of track built specifically to get passengers between the airport and central London without competing with ordinary commuter traffic — part of why the 15–20 minute run to Paddington still feels almost suspiciously fast for a city the size of London. Building a private, airport-only rail spur was a genuinely unusual infrastructure decision at the time; most cities make airport trains share track with everyone else.

It runs on the same Great Western main line corridor that Isambard Kingdom Brunel engineered in the 1830s, one of the first great trunk railways in the world and still one of the most important rail corridors in Britain — the family's train to Edinburgh later this trip uses a different line entirely, but the same basic 19th-century engineering philosophy of building fast, direct routes between major cities.

The Elizabeth line, opened in 2022 after years of delay as the Crossrail project, now offers a slower but considerably cheaper alternative covering the same route — a genuinely new piece of London infrastructure built more than a century after Brunel's original railway, on a corridor the city had been trying to build for the better part of two decades.

Hyde Park & Kensington Gardens

Read the full story

Hyde Park began as Henry VIII's private hunting ground after he seized the land from Westminster Abbey in 1536; Charles I opened it to the public a century later, in 1637, making it one of the oldest public parks in the world by royal decree rather than public campaign. Queen Caroline reshaped the landscape in the 1730s and dammed the Westbourne stream to create the Serpentine, the lake that now marks the informal boundary between Hyde Park and the more formally landscaped Kensington Gardens next door.

The two parks together cover almost 350 acres in the middle of one of the densest cities on Earth — proof that London planned room to breathe centuries before anyone used that phrase. Hyde Park has also hosted its share of history beyond royal leisure: the Great Exhibition of 1851 was staged here inside the Crystal Palace, a vast iron-and-glass structure built specifically for the occasion and later moved and eventually destroyed by fire in 1936.

Speakers' Corner, in the park's northeast corner, has hosted unregulated public speech and debate since at least the 1870s — anyone can turn up and speak on anything, a tradition connected to the site's earlier, darker history as the location of the Tyburn gallows, where condemned prisoners were historically allowed a final public speech before execution.

Sky Garden

Read the full story

Sky Garden occupies the top three floors of 20 Fenchurch Street, the tower Londoners nicknamed the "Walkie-Talkie" for the way it widens as it rises — an unusual shape architect Rafael Viñoly designed specifically to maximize the rentable floor area on the upper stories, since higher floors command higher rent regardless of a building's shape. The original 2005 proposal was considerably taller, at 722 feet, before the design was cut down to its current 525 feet over concerns it would block protected sightlines toward St Paul's Cathedral.

The building's shape caused a genuine engineering scandal before it even opened: its concave south face reflected sunlight intensely enough in 2013 to melt parts of a parked Jaguar and scorch a shopfront carpet across the street, a fiasco the tabloids immediately nicknamed the "Walkie Scorchie." A permanent sunshade solved the problem before the tower fully opened, and the building went on to win the 2015 Carbuncle Cup — a tongue-in-cheek British architecture prize for the year's ugliest new building, awarded by the same industry that also uses the tower as a case study in ambitious, controversial design.

Sky Garden opened in 2015 as one of London's only free, publicly accessible viewing spaces at height, a deliberate trade the developer made for planning permission after the project was initially denied — the city eventually allowed the extra height and unconventional shape specifically in exchange for a public garden anyone could visit without paying admission. On a clear evening it gives an unbroken view across the Thames, the City's older domes and spires, and the newer glass skyline crowding in around them.

Twenty-five years sounds young until someone points out that it is one-quarter of a century. That description makes Kea sound as though she should have an archival wing, a preservation plan and perhaps a small brass plaque. She has none of those things. She does, however, get to celebrate this birthday in London with the three people who have known her the longest.

Her story began while the country was still trying to understand the attacks of September 11. Nurses gathered in the hospital room to watch television updates while Mom waited for Kea to arrive. Outside that room, people were carrying fear, grief and uncertainty. Inside it, our family was waiting for someone whose strength, confidence and personality would eventually make a quiet entrance seem highly unlikely.

In the middle of so much fear and sadness, Kea arrived and gave our family something beautiful to hold onto.

The day also produced another family story, although Jay probably would not have volunteered for his role in it. While Mom and newborn Kea remained at the hospital, Dad took Jay home for a while. Jay was 19 months old by then — long past the carrier stage — and walking up the driveway with Dad when the slope got the better of him. He stumbled, lost his footing, and bumped his head. Everyone survived, but Jay and Dad still had some explaining to do to Mom back at the hospital the next day.

Kea has never been short on presence. She is strong, direct and willing to stand up for herself. She does not put up with nonsense—including Dad's—and she is one of the few people who can make him laugh when he is angry at the world and has decided joy is temporarily unavailable. Sometimes all it takes is: “Hook, jab, uppercut, boom-boom. Just kidding, boom-boom's not real.” It should not work as well as it does, but neither should half of our family humor.

She cheered, played golf, joined a sorority, earned two Arizona State University degrees and built an accounting career of her own. She also spent countless hours researching this journey and creating the spreadsheet that turned a collection of possibilities into a trip the four of us could actually take. London may provide the setting for her birthday, but her work helped bring all of us here.

Kea and Dad are alike enough to understand one another and alike enough to drive one another completely crazy. Anyone who has watched them attempt an escape room already understands the operational risk. They can argue, compete, give each other a hard time and occasionally discover that placing both of them in charge of the same problem was a tactical mistake. Beneath all of that is a relationship filled with humor, respect and a great deal of love.

At home, Mango and Pearle will observe the occasion by continuing to contribute absolutely nothing financially. Kea gave both rescued cats a home, and they have repaid her by becoming deeply loved members of her little family while remaining completely unemployed.

This chapter includes London history, birthday brunch above the city, a West End matinee, Trafalgar Square, St James's and dinner in Soho. But the landmarks are not the center of the day. Kea is.

Twenty-five years is the history of a daughter becoming a woman Dad genuinely admires: strong, intelligent, funny, caring and entirely herself. This birthday gives the four of us a chance to stop, look back at everything that brought her here and celebrate who she has become. Dad was grateful for her when she entered that hospital room. A quarter of a century later, he is even more grateful for the relationship they share, the laughter she brings into his life and the privilege of being her dad.

Trafalgar Square & Nelson's Column

Read the full story

Trafalgar Square was built in the 1840s on the site of the old Royal Mews, specifically to commemorate the Battle of Trafalgar and hold Nelson's Column — not the other way around. Architect William Railton won a public competition to design the column, which was built between 1840 and 1843 from Dartmoor granite topped with an 18-foot sandstone statue of Admiral Nelson, carved by Edward Hodges Baily. The whole monument stands 169 feet tall, and a crew of fourteen reportedly ate a dinner balanced on top of the column's base just before the statue was hoisted into place.

The four bronze lions guarding the base weren't added until 1867, sculpted by Sir Edwin Landseer — better known as a painter than a sculptor — and cast from the bronze of captured French cannons. Local legend holds the lions will roar if Big Ben ever strikes thirteen. The square's northwest corner, the "Fourth Plinth," sat empty from 1841 until 1998; it's now used for a rotating series of contemporary art commissions rather than a permanent statue, which makes it worth a glance every time you pass through — it's never quite the same square twice.

Liberty London

Read the full story

Liberty was founded in 1875 by Arthur Lasenby Liberty, who started by importing ornamental silks and objects from Japan and the East at a moment when "Orientalist" design was suddenly fashionable across London. The store became so associated with a particular decorative style that "Liberty print" is still used generically today for a certain kind of small floral fabric pattern, regardless of who actually made it.

The mock-Tudor building on Great Marlborough Street — the one you'll actually walk past today — wasn't built until the 1920s, and its timbers were reclaimed from two decommissioned Royal Navy warships, HMS Impregnable and HMS Hindustan, deliberately chosen to give a brand-new building the weight of genuine maritime history. It remains one of the more theatrical pieces of retail architecture in London, built to look centuries older than it actually is.

Liberty's fabric department became so influential in the late 19th and early 20th centuries that the store is credited with helping popularize the Arts and Crafts movement and, later, Art Nouveau design across Britain — in Italy, Art Nouveau is still sometimes called "stile Liberty" after the London store that helped spread it.

Yauatcha opened in 2004, founded by restaurateur Alan Yau — the same person behind Wagamama and Hakkasan — as a more casual, all-day dim sum teahouse built around a ground-floor patisserie case and a moodier basement dining room with tropical fish tanks and a view into the working kitchen. For anyone who hasn't had it: dim sum is a Cantonese tradition of small, shareable plates — mostly steamed or fried dumplings, buns, and rolls — meant to be ordered a few at a time and passed around the table rather than eaten as one big plate to yourself. It began as a teahouse snack in southern China and has since become one of the most social ways to eat in the world, which is part of why it suited a birthday table so well.

The Michelin star itself comes from anonymous inspectors working for the tire company Michelin, who started rating restaurants in 1900 to encourage road trips (and tire sales). Restaurants are visited unannounced, sometimes repeatedly over months or years, and judged purely on the food — one star means "a very good restaurant in its category," two means "excellent cooking, worth a detour," and three, the rarest, means "exceptional cuisine, worth a special journey." Stars can also be taken away if standards slip, which is what makes Yauatcha holding one for twenty consecutive years genuinely unusual.

Executive Head Chef Tong Chee Hwee designed the original menu and still oversees it across the wider Hakkasan Group; all of the kitchen's equipment, down to the knives, was shipped over from Hong Kong at opening specifically to keep the dumplings authentic. Twenty years and one very loud family birthday dinner later, it's still doing the same job it always did.

Alan Yau's broader career reshaped how London eats: Wagamama, which he founded in 1992, helped introduce fast, communal-table Japanese noodle dining to a British public that had barely encountered it before, and Hakkasan — Yauatcha's more formal older sibling — became the first Chinese restaurant in the UK to earn a Michelin star, a full three years before Yauatcha followed suit.

Westminster Abbey: A Thousand Years of Coronations

Read the full story

The site's religious history goes back further than most people assume — legend holds that a Saxon king converted to Christianity and built a church here sometime between 500 and 600 AD, and a small Benedictine community was documented on this ground, then called Thorney Island, by around 960. Edward the Confessor is the figure who actually made the site matter: in the 1040s he built his royal palace beside the Thames right next to the existing monastery, and spent years of his reign and treasury building an extravagant new abbey church in the Norman style. It was consecrated on 28 December 1065 — Edward, gravely ill, couldn't attend his own church's dedication, and died just over a week later. He was buried in front of the altar of the church he'd just finished.

The coronation that started everything. Later that same year, William the Conqueror chose to be crowned at Westminster on Christmas Day 1066, a calculated move to link his new reign to Edward's sanctified memory. It worked almost too well: every English and British monarch since has been crowned here, with only two exceptions in nearly a thousand years (Edward V, who was never crowned at all before his disappearance in the Tower, and Edward VIII, who abdicated before his coronation could happen). The current Coronation Chair, built in 1300–1301, has been used at nearly every coronation since — it was even built specifically to enclose the Stone of Scone, seized from Scotland and used to crown Scottish kings for centuries before that.

Henry III's rebuild. The Gothic church visible today isn't Edward's building — it's Henry III's. In 1245, deliberately competing with the great Gothic cathedrals rising across France at the same moment (Amiens, Chartres, Reims), Henry tore down most of Edward's Norman church and rebuilt it from the crossing eastward in the new French-influenced Gothic style, complete with ribbed vaulting and flying buttresses to support taller ceilings and bigger windows. The rebuilt church was consecrated in October 1269, and Henry III personally moved Edward the Confessor's remains to an even more elaborate shrine at its center — a shrine that still exists today.

Poets' Corner and the people beyond royalty. Geoffrey Chaucer was buried in the Abbey's south transept in 1400 — not, originally, because he was a famous poet, but because he'd worked as Clerk of Works at the Palace of Westminster nearby. His grave nonetheless became the seed of what's now called Poets' Corner, where more than a hundred writers are buried or memorialized, including Shakespeare, Dickens, Jane Austen, and the Brontë sisters. Thirty kings and queens are buried in the Abbey altogether, the last being George II in 1760 — every monarch since has been interred at Windsor instead, making Westminster Abbey primarily a coronation church today rather than a royal cemetery.

The towers you'll actually see. The famous west towers framing the entrance weren't part of either Edward's or Henry's building — they weren't completed until 1745, designed by Nicholas Hawksmoor nearly five centuries after the rest of the church. The "medieval" skyline everyone photographs is, in that specific sense, an 18th-century addition sitting on top of a much older building, though Hawksmoor matched the Gothic style closely enough that almost no visitor ever notices the seam.

Churchill War Rooms

Read the full story

Construction on the Cabinet War Rooms began in 1938, as war with Germany looked increasingly inevitable, and the complex became fully operational on 27 August 1939 — literally a week before Britain declared war. Built beneath the Treasury building in Whitehall, the rooms housed Churchill's war cabinet and military planning staff through the entire Blitz, safe under a reinforced concrete slab that could withstand a direct hit from the bombs being dropped on London above.

The complex ran around the clock for six years: senior ministers, typists, military planners, and Churchill himself worked in cramped, poorly ventilated rooms lit by exposed bulbs, tracking the war's progress on wall maps still marked with the pinholes from years of moving pins across them. Churchill made several of his wartime broadcasts from a room here disguised as his own private office, complete with a hidden hotline connecting directly to President Roosevelt.

The complex was abandoned the moment Japan surrendered in August 1945 — lights switched off, papers left on desks, clocks stopped at the exact moment work ended — and it sat almost untouched for decades, preserved less by design than by simple neglect, until the Imperial War Museum took over its administration in the early 1980s and opened it to the public. Walking through today, you're seeing rooms genuinely frozen at the moment the war ended, not a later recreation.

Tower Bridge

Read the full story

Tower Bridge opened in 1894 after eight years of construction, built to solve a genuine civic problem: London Bridge, the only crossing downstream at the time, was overwhelmed with traffic, but any new bridge close to the Tower of London had to allow tall sailing ships to keep reaching the busy docks upriver. The solution, designed by Horace Jones and engineer John Wolfe Barry, was a bascule bridge — one that opens in the middle — dressed in Victorian Gothic stonework specifically so it wouldn't visually clash with the medieval Tower right next to it.

Underneath that stonework is a full Victorian steel skeleton, a 19th-century engineering disguise that let architects have both the modern hydraulic mechanism and the historic look London's planners insisted on. Six steam-powered pumping engines originally drove the massive bascules; two of those original engines are preserved on-site and still run for demonstrations, even though the bridge itself switched to an electric hydraulic system in 1976. The bridge still lifts roughly 800 times a year and over 40,000 people cross it daily.

It has survived both World Wars intact despite sitting directly in the flight path of German bombers targeting the docks during the Blitz, and it has hosted its share of stunts and stories since — including a 1952 incident where a London bus driver had to accelerate across a gap that had already begun opening beneath him, successfully jumping the bus to the other bascule before it rose too high to clear.

The Longer Story

Musselburgh Old Links has a serious claim to being the oldest golf course still in use in the world, with recorded play from at least the mid-1600s — well before the Old Course at St Andrews took its modern shape. Playing it today with hickory clubs is less a novelty than a small act of historical accuracy.

King's Cross, the East Coast Main Line, and Waverley

Read the full story

King's Cross station opened in 1852, designed by Lewis Cubitt with a deliberately plain brick facade meant to let the two great train-shed arches behind it do the talking — a striking contrast to the ornate Gothic pile that its rival St Pancras built right next door a couple of decades later. The East Coast Main Line running north from King's Cross dates to the 1850s and has carried express trains between London and Edinburgh for well over a century and a half.

The Flying Scotsman name made the route famous starting in the 1920s, when it referred to a specific named express service (and later, confusingly, to a specific locomotive that still runs on heritage lines today). The train the family takes north follows the same corridor, at considerably higher speed. Waverley station, the destination — tucked into a valley below the Old Town, wedged between the Old and New Towns almost by accident of geography — has been Edinburgh's main terminus since 1868, named for Sir Walter Scott's Waverley novels rather than the other way around.

King's Cross today is as well known for fiction as for real trains — the famous "Platform 9¾" from the Harry Potter novels is staged just outside the station with a half-embedded luggage trolley for photos, drawing long lines of visitors who've never actually caught a train there. The station's real claim to modern fame is quieter but more significant: a major 2012 redevelopment gave it the largest single-span station roof in Europe, a sweeping lattice canopy built over the same Victorian brick facade Cubitt designed more than 160 years earlier.

Musselburgh Old Links: Playing Inside History

Read the full story

Today the four of you play Musselburgh together with hickory clubs — the same basic implements golfers here used more than a century ago, on ground where the earliest confirmed record of golf being played anywhere in the world was written down. The entry appears in the account book of Sir John Foulis of Ravelston, dated 2 March 1672. It is sometimes claimed that Mary, Queen of Scots played nearby at Seton even earlier, in 1567, though that story rests on tradition rather than a document anyone can point to.

The racecourse that surrounds the golf course. Musselburgh Links sits almost entirely inside a working horse racing track — not next to it, but inside it, sharing the same ground. The first organized races at Musselburgh ran in 1777 under the Royal Caledonian Hunt, and the racecourse as it exists today was built around the golf links in 1816. Golfers duck under the rails between holes; the course only closes to golf on the handful of days each year when the horses are actually running. It is a genuinely unusual arrangement — two very different sports sharing one plot of land for over two hundred years — and it has occasionally put the golf course at risk, most seriously in 1986 and 1996, when proposals to widen and modernize the racetrack threatened to swallow the 1st green.

The Guinness World Records story. Musselburgh held the official Guinness World Records title of "oldest golf course in the world" as recently as 2009, built on that 1672 documentary evidence. The record did not survive a rival claim from St Andrews, whose Archbishop Hamilton's Charter — signed in 1552 — turned out to be an even earlier piece of paper. The charter itself has nothing to do with golf directly; it is principally a license allowing the Archbishop to breed rabbits on the links, with a clause tacked on permitting the local population to keep "playing at golf, futball, schuting at all gamis." Guinness reassigned the title to St Andrews on the strength of that document, and it has stayed there since — a reminder that "oldest" in golf often comes down to whichever scrap of paper survived, not necessarily whoever swung a club first. Musselburgh's own claim is arguably the stronger one for continuous, unchanged design: the routing you'll play today is essentially the same nine holes the course has used since the 19th century, while St Andrews has been reshaped repeatedly over the centuries.

Course design and the Park family. Musselburgh was originally seven holes. An eighth was added in 1832, and the ninth and final hole came in 1870, giving the course the routing it plays today. Much of that development ran through one family: Willie Park Sr., the first-ever Open Champion in 1860, was a Musselburgh man, and his son Willie Park Jr. — who also won the Open twice, in 1887 and 1889 — became one of the most influential golf course architects in history, later designing Sunningdale and Huntercombe in England and dozens of courses in the United States. A detail from Musselburgh's maintenance shed quietly became a global standard: the hole cutter in use here measured 4.25 inches across, and the Royal & Ancient eventually adopted that exact dimension as the official size of a golf hole everywhere in the world. The green immediately off the first tee once bordered Mrs. Forman's, a pub that cut a serving hatch directly onto the course so golfers could get a drink without leaving play — a small, very Scottish solution to a very Scottish problem.

The Open Championship at Musselburgh. Between 1874 and 1889, the Open rotated among three courses — St Andrews, Prestwick, and Musselburgh — and Musselburgh hosted it six times before the Honourable Company of Edinburgh Golfers, who were based here, relocated to build Muirfield and took the Championship with them.

The Longer Story

St Andrews earned the title Home of Golf the honest way: by being where the game's rules, courses, and institutions took their modern shape over centuries, one links at a time, long before anyone thought to trademark the phrase. The town's cathedral ruins and Swilcan Bridge carry roughly as much history as the golf does.

Gullane No. 2: The Course You're Playing Today

Read the full story

Golf has been played on the ground around Gullane since at least 1650, but the course the family tees off on this morning — Gullane No. 2 — is a much more deliberate piece of design work. It was laid out in 1898 by Willie Park Jr., the same architect whose family shaped Musselburgh a generation earlier. Park won the Open Championship twice himself, in 1887 and 1889, before turning to course design full-time and becoming one of the most sought-after architects of his era — Sunningdale's Old Course and Huntercombe in England both carry his name, and he later designed dozens of courses across the United States, including Olympia Fields outside Chicago.

Where it sits, and how it's built. Gullane Golf Club actually has three separate 18-hole courses — No. 1, No. 2, and No. 3 — sharing the same stretch of linksland above the village, with Muirfield and Luffness New golf clubs as neighbors just down the road. No. 2 runs parallel to the older No. 1 course for its first seven holes before breaking off toward a nature reserve near Aberlady Bay and turning back for home. It plays 6,396 yards to a par of 71, threading through 107 bunkers cut into the natural humps and hollows of the linksland, with bent grass fairways and fescue greens averaging about 500 square meters each — firm, fast, and exactly the texture links golf is supposed to have.

The signature holes. No. 2 is best known for its short par-3s, and the standout is the 11th — 215 yards, played into the wind off the Firth of Forth, with views considered among the best on the property. The course balances that with real length elsewhere: the 6th and 16th are both par-5s stretching past 490 yards. From the higher points of the course, the views reach across the Firth of Forth toward Edinburgh, Fife, and on a clear day the coastline well beyond — the same water the family will fly over in reverse in ten days' time.

Championship pedigree. No. 2 has hosted Open Championship Final Qualifying — including in 1980 — along with the Seniors Open Amateur Championship in 2000, the Scottish Amateur Championship, and the Ladies British Open Amateur Championship, each on multiple occasions. National Club Golfer has ranked it 39th among all courses in Scotland, a genuinely difficult list to crack in a country with this much competition for the honor.

The neighbor next door. Down the hill from Gullane sits Muirfield, home of the Honourable Company of Edinburgh Golfers — the same club that hosted the Open at Musselburgh six times before relocating here in 1891. It's a small, tidy piece of golf history: the club that once played Open golf on the ground you played yesterday now plays it a five-minute drive from the ground you're playing today.

The Courses Surrounding the Old Course

Read the full story

The Old Course is only one of seven courses the St Andrews Links Trust operates on this same stretch of ground — a public, non-profit trust that has run the whole complex since the town council reacquired the links in 1894, specifically to keep them open to everyone rather than a private club. Between them, these seven courses host well over 230,000 rounds a year.

The New Course (1895). Despite the name, it's the second-oldest course on the property, built by Old Tom Morris to relieve pressure on the Old Course as golf's popularity exploded in the late 19th century. It runs alongside its older neighbor and shares its reputation for tight, gorse-lined fairways — many good judges rate it a harder test than the Old Course itself.

The Jubilee Course (1897). Built in a single season to open on the public holiday marking Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee, it started as a modest 12-hole layout for beginners and women golfers. It didn't become the full 18-hole championship course it is today until a 1988 redesign by Donald Steel — the same architect who later worked on protecting Musselburgh from the racecourse expansion.

The Eden Course (1914). Tucked along the Eden Estuary and built as a classic out-and-back links, the Eden is the local favorite among the town's own golfers — less famous than its neighbors, but well regarded for exactly that reason.

Strathtyrum and Balgove. Strathtyrum is a shorter, gentler course built for golfers who want links golf without the Old Course's teeth, and Balgove is a 9-hole, par-30 course built specifically for beginners and juniors — the entry point for a huge number of golfers who went on to play everywhere else on this list.

The Castle Course (2008). The newest addition, and the only one not built on the flat linksland shared by the other six — it sits on clifftops on the far side of town, with views many golfers rate as the best of any course here, the Old Course included.

The Himalayas: St Andrews' Other Putting Green

Read the full story

Before 1860, caddies waiting for a bag laid out a rough putting patch near where the Rusacks Hotel stands today, just to pass the time between jobs. A handful of local women began putting there too, and as the game grew more crowded, they were moved to a separate stretch of rough ground north of the Swilcan Burn — respectable recreation options for young ladies in Victorian St Andrews were otherwise limited to croquet, archery, and battledore.

Old Tom Morris built it, twice. In 1867, Old Tom laid out a fifteen-hole "miniature links" using nothing but a cleek and a putter, and that green became the founding ground of the St Andrews Ladies' Golf Club — the world's first ladies-only golf club. He served as the green's keeper until he retired in 1895, and was made an honorary member for his trouble. In 1893, the club acquired more land to the north; the new ground was dramatically hilly compared to the original patch, and the green picked up the name it's kept ever since: the Himalayas.

Still run by the ladies, not the Links Trust. Unlike the Old Course and its six siblings, the Himalayas isn't operated by the St Andrews Links Trust at all — it's still run by the St Andrews Ladies' Putting Club, the same organization Old Tom helped found in 1867. An old fisherman's path once cut straight through the green, flooding regularly enough that ladies needed planks laid down to cross it; the path is paved over today but is still called "Jordan," the name it was given more than a century ago.

Everyone has played it, nobody takes it seriously. Old Tom Morris, Bobby Jones, Jack Nicklaus, and Tiger Woods have all putted across the Himalayas' wild contours at one point or another, usually laughing more than competing. That's always been the point — the green was never built to crown a champion, only to teach touch, patience, and a sense of humor with a putter in hand. It's open to the public for a modest fee, ages six and up, and remains, by a wide margin, the cheapest fun in St Andrews.

The R&A World Golf Museum

Read the full story

The museum sits directly across the road from the Royal & Ancient Clubhouse, close enough to the Old Course's 1st tee that you could genuinely watch a group play out while standing on the front steps. It began life humbly, as little more than a cabinet of curiosities the R&A kept in the 1800s, before the Club funded a proper building through a dedicated charitable trust in 1985. The British Golf Museum, as it was then known, opened to the public on 25 June 1990.

From British Golf Museum to World Golf Museum. The museum closed for a year-plus renovation and reopened in June 2021 under its current name, redisplayed with six new thematic zones and immersive video and interactive exhibits — a deliberate attempt to make the collection land with newcomers as well as golf obsessives. It also now houses the Alastair J Johnston Library, nearly 30,000 golf books gifted by the collector and longtime IMG vice-chairman, making it the largest golf literature collection anywhere in the world.

What's actually inside. The collection documents the game from medieval times to the present — historic clubs and balls, championship trophies, and the equipment and memorabilia of both the men's and women's games. Part of the collection originally belonged to the Women Golfers' Museum, founded in London in 1939 and folded in decades later. Past exhibitions have included a dedicated retrospective on Seve Ballesteros's three Open wins (1979, 1984, 1988), timed to run through the 150th Open at St Andrews in 2022.

The building nobody but members can enter. The R&A Clubhouse itself, visible from the museum's windows, is open to the public exactly one day a year. The original Claret Jug — first awarded in 1928, permanently on display since — has never left the building since it arrived; every Open winner since receives a replica to keep, while the real jug stays home in St Andrews.

The Old Course: How the World's Most Famous Golf Course Got That Way

Read the full story

You played it, or at least walked it, yesterday afternoon. Before this final St Andrews morning ends, it's worth understanding exactly what you were standing on — because nearly every convention modern golf takes for granted was set, more or less by accident, on this specific piece of linksland.

No one designed it — it evolved. Unlike Gullane No. 2 or the modern additions to Musselburgh, the Old Course was never laid out on a blank sheet by a single architect. Golf has been played on this ground since at least the 1400s, and the course simply accumulated its shape over centuries — sheep grazing paths, natural dunes, and the routing golfers wore into the land all played a part before anyone thought to call it "design." The genius everyone credits to the Old Course — blind shots, shared fairways, greens the size of small parks — largely comes from terrain that shaped itself, not from a plan.

Why golf is played in rounds of 18. The Old Course originally had 22 holes, played out along a single strip of linksland and back again, with only two holes not shared. In 1764, the club that would become the Royal & Ancient combined the first four short holes into two, turning the round into 18 holes each way — 18 out, 18 back. That number had no particular significance before this; it became the standard for every golf course built anywhere in the world afterward, purely because St Andrews happened to set it first.

Old Tom Morris finished the job. Tom Morris — the same man the breakfast restaurant this morning is named for — served as Keeper of the Greens at St Andrews from 1865 until his death in 1908, after an earlier career as a club and ball maker and a four-time Open champion. In 1863 he separated the shared 1st and 17th holes, finalizing the routing that exists today: seven of the Old Course's greens are shared "double greens" serving two holes at once, with only four single greens on the entire course — one of them the tiny, exposed green on the Road Hole, the 17th, that sits beside the very hotel you'll leave this morning.

The record the Old Course still holds. The Open Championship has been played at St Andrews more times than at any other course in the rotation — the tournament first came here in 1873, and it has returned roughly thirty times since, most recently in 2022 for the 150th Open. No other Open venue comes close to that total. Combined with the Women's British Open, the Senior Open, and the Dunhill Cup, St Andrews carries more championship golf history than anywhere else in the world.

The Road Hole: Golf's Cruelest 495 Yards

Read the full story

You played it, or at least stood on it, yesterday afternoon. The 17th at the Old Course — the Road Hole — is widely considered the hardest single hole in championship golf, and visually it has barely changed in well over a century: a blind tee shot fired over the corner of a hotel, an impossibly narrow green perched diagonally beside an actual public road, and a bunker so feared it has its own nickname. Par dropped from 5 to 4 over the decades even as the hole got longer — one of the only real changes to a layout that otherwise looks almost identical to photographs from 1910.

The blind tee shot over a hotel. The tee shot plays directly over outbuildings at the corner of what's now the Old Course Hotel, with out-of-bounds running the entire right side. Those outbuildings aren't decorative — they're a deliberate replica of the old railway sheds that genuinely stood on that exact spot, once used to store coal for the St Andrews Links railway line before it was dismantled around 1969. When the original sheds were finally torn down and the Old Course Hotel went up in 1968, the hole briefly lost its famous blind shot entirely — golfers could simply see over the corner. It didn't last: after a few Open Championships played without that obstruction, replica sheds were deliberately rebuilt in the mid-1980s specifically to restore the blind tee shot, because by then it was considered too essential to the hole's character to lose. The "OLD COURSE HOTEL" lettering painted across the shed became the aiming point almost by accident of geography, and players still argue over which letter to aim at depending on their shot shape — fade players lean toward the "O," draw players toward the "H." Will Zalatoris once said you can hit two wildly different shots at that same logo and end up in nearly the same spot, which tells you everything about how little room there actually is to work with.

"The Sands of Nakajima." In 1978, Japanese golfer Tommy Nakajima was in genuine contention at the Open when his ball found the small, steep bunker guarding the front-left of the green. He needed four separate swings just to get out. The British press nicknamed the bunker "The Sands of Nakajima" that week, and the name has stuck for nearly fifty years — most golfers who play the hole today have no idea who Nakajima actually was, only that his name is permanently attached to the most feared piece of sand in golf.

Tom Watson's wall, 1984. Tied for the lead with Seve Ballesteros in the final round of the 1984 Open, Watson pushed a 2-iron approach clean over the green — and the ground behind the green isn't rough, it's a paved road backed by a stone wall. His ball came to rest hard against that wall, leaving him almost no room to swing. The resulting bogey cost him what would have been a third consecutive Claret Jug; Ballesteros won instead, pumping his fist on the 18th green in one of golf's most photographed celebrations.

Costantino Rocca's save, and Daly's Jug, 1995. In regulation play, John Daly — then leading by one — found the Road Hole bunker and escaped with a bogey. An hour later, Costantino Rocca faced nearly the same shot in a four-hole playoff against Daly. He needed two attempts to get out, handing the Claret Jug to Daly. It's a cruel footnote to one of the best shots ever played at the hole: earlier that same Open, Rocca had held Daly off by draining an enormous, snaking putt off the road itself on the 72nd hole just to force the playoff in the first place.

David Duval's "abominable snowman," 2000. Playing alongside Tiger Woods in the final round, Duval hooked a 5-iron approach hard into the face of the Road Hole bunker from 181 yards out. It took him four shots to escape. The resulting score — an 8 on a par-4 — was dubbed an "abominable snowman" by commentators for the shape its digits made on the leaderboard, and it dropped Duval from a tie for second all the way down to 11th place in a single hole.

The Jigger Inn. A small pub sits right beside the green, close enough to hear the groans — the Jigger Inn, built in 1852 as the stationmaster's lodge for the old St Andrews Links railway station, back when trains ran directly behind what's now the 17th green. The railway is long gone, but the Jigger has stayed exactly where it always was, now serving pub food and pints to golfers walking off the most punishing hole they'll play all week.

Borough Market

Read the full story

Borough Market has traded on roughly this site since at least the 12th century, and the market itself claims origins as far back as 1014, making it one of London's oldest continuously operating markets — trading long before anyone called food shopping an experience. For most of its history it was a wholesale market rather than the retail food destination it's become; the shift toward artisan producers and prepared food happened gradually through the 1990s and 2000s.

The current wrought-iron and glass market buildings mostly date to the 1850s, built when the market relocated slightly south of its medieval location to make way for London Bridge station's expansion. The market suffered a genuine tragedy in June 2017, when it was forced to close for eleven days following a terror attack nearby on London Bridge — a closure that hit the small independent traders hardest, and one the market's own history now records alongside the Great Fire of 1666 and wartime bombing as one of the disruptions it has had to recover from over roughly a thousand years of continuous trading.

Tate Modern

Read the full story

Tate Modern occupies the former Bankside Power Station, a mid-century industrial building designed by Giles Gilbert Scott — the same architect behind the classic red British telephone box — and originally built to generate electricity for London between 1947 and 1963, then decommissioned in 1981. The building sat empty for over a decade before architects Herzog & de Meuron converted it into a museum, opening in 2000 as one of the largest museums of modern art in the world.

Its scale — including the vast Turbine Hall, big enough to house room-sized art installations that would be impossible almost anywhere else — was inherited directly from its power-station days; the building's original 99-meter chimney remains the tallest structure on the South Bank skyline. It draws several million visitors a year, free of charge for the permanent collection, funded instead through special exhibitions and donations.

The Turbine Hall commissions have become genuine cultural events in their own right — past installations have included a giant crack running the length of the floor, a room-filling sun made of mist and mirrors that visitors lay beneath by the thousands, and enormous slides people could actually ride down. A 2012 extension, the Blavatnik Building, added another ten stories of gallery space built from perforated brick designed to echo the original power station's industrial brick shell.

The Millennium Bridge

Read the full story

The Millennium Bridge opened in June 2000 as London's first new Thames crossing in over a century, designed by architect Norman Foster, engineer Arup, and sculptor Anthony Caro as a deliberately minimal "blade of light" — a flat steel span with no visible towers or high cables, unlike almost every other bridge in London.

It had to close within days of opening after pedestrians caused it to sway alarmingly from side to side, a phenomenon engineers hadn't fully anticipated called synchronous lateral excitation — essentially, the bridge's natural sway caused people to unconsciously adjust their footsteps in matching rhythm, which made the sway worse. The fix, installing tuned mass dampers to absorb that motion, took nearly two years and has become a textbook case study in structural engineering taught in universities worldwide. The bridge reopened in 2002 and has been rock-solid ever since.

Londoners nicknamed it the "Wobbly Bridge" almost immediately, a name that's stuck in casual conversation even now, a quarter-century after the wobble itself was engineered away — a rare case of a structure's early embarrassment becoming part of its permanent identity rather than something anyone bothered trying to live down.

The London Eye

The London Eye opened on New Year's Eve 1999 (to the public in March 2000) as one of several millennium landmark projects funded to mark the year 2000, and was, for over a decade, the tallest Ferris wheel in the world at 135 meters — a title eventually taken by wheels in Singapore and Las Vegas. Harrods: From Tea Shop to Qatari Landmark

Read the full story

Harrods began in 1834 when Charles Henry Harrod opened a small grocery and tea-trading shop in London's East End, moving to the current Knightsbridge site in 1849. It survived a catastrophic fire in 1883 that destroyed the entire store just before Christmas — the story goes that Harrod honored every existing customer order that year regardless of the fire, which cemented the store's reputation for service almost overnight.

Today's ornate terracotta building, designed by architect C. W. Stephens — who also designed Claridge's and Harvey Nichols — was constructed between 1894 and 1905 and now covers roughly five acres of retail floor across seven floors, one of the largest department stores in the world with more than a million square feet of selling space.

The ownership saga. The original company listed on the London Stock Exchange in 1889, was acquired by House of Fraser in 1959, and then in 1985 was bought by the Fayed brothers — most notably Mohamed Al-Fayed, an Egyptian businessman who'd built a fortune in shipping, property, and hotels (including the Ritz Paris) before adding Harrods to his portfolio. Al-Fayed owned the store for 25 years, becoming one of London's most colorful and controversial business figures, before selling it to the Qatar Investment Authority — Qatar's sovereign wealth fund — in 2010, ending British family ownership of the store entirely.

The Christmas Shop on the 4th floor runs year-round regardless of season, a deliberately strange, slightly theatrical detail that's become one of the store's best-known quirks — a small piece of the same showmanship that's defined Harrods since Charles Harrod first decided a grocery shop could also be a destination.

Wembley Stadium

Read the full story

The original Wembley Stadium — officially the Empire Stadium — opened in 1923 for the British Empire Exhibition, built on the site of an abandoned Victorian folly called Watkin's Tower. Its famous twin white towers, 126 feet tall and built of ferro-concrete, became instantly recognizable symbols of English football. The stadium's opening event, the 1923 FA Cup Final, drew a crowd so far beyond its official 127,000 capacity — some estimates run well past 200,000 — that it's remembered as the "White Horse Final," named for the police horse that helped clear thousands of spectators off the actual pitch before kickoff could happen at all.

That original stadium, and its beloved Twin Towers, were demolished in 2003 after 80 years of service; the towers had been Grade II listed since 1976, which didn't stop them coming down to make way for the new build. The current Wembley opened in 2007, replacing them with the now-signature arch — 133 meters tall, partly structural (it helps support the retractable roof) and visible from parts of London more than 10 miles away.

The rebuilt stadium has already set records across an unusually wide range of events: 98,000 for an Adele concert in 2017, over 98,000 for a 2024 Anthony Joshua boxing match, and NFL crowds regularly exceeding 86,000 for the London games hosted here since 2007. This is the first time the venue has ever hosted a college football game, and the Union Jack Classic is designed to be an annual fixture going forward — meaning this particular first is one that won't be repeated the same way twice.

Sixes Cricket: A Very New Way to Play a Very Old Game

Read the full story

Sixes Social Cricket is a genuinely recent invention — founded in London in 2020 by a group whose background was in restaurants and hospitality, not professional cricket. The idea was to take the single most exciting moment in the sport — a "six," when a batter hits the ball out of the ground on the full for the maximum score — and build an entire venue around chasing that feeling, without needing a pitch, a full team, or any prior experience at all.

How it actually works. Players bat inside enclosed, simulator-style nets fitted with sensor and tracking technology (the same category of tech used in golf simulators) that reads bat speed, contact, and trajectory to score each shot automatically — no umpires required, and no real ball ever leaves the cage. It's built specifically so a group with zero cricket background can walk in, get a five-minute crash course, and be competitively swinging within the hour.

Part cricket, part sports bar. The company now runs venues across the UK, with its flagship locations — including this one — pairing the cricket bays with a full bar and a genuine night-out atmosphere; the founders have said openly that the hospitality side came first and the cricket followed. The Wembley location sits inside BOXPARK Wembley, London's largest covered street food venue, with more than two dozen independent food traders under one roof — meaning tonight's "dinner" question mostly solves itself once the bats are put down.

Why cricket, and why now. The format taps into the same wave of British interest driving The Hundred and other short, TV-friendly cricket formats — stripping the sport down to its most explosive moment and repackaging it as a social night out rather than a five-day Test match. For a family of lifelong ASU sports fans with no real cricket history of their own, it's about as level a playing field as a competitive evening gets: nobody in this group has ever swung at a cricket ball before tonight.

The Union Jack Classic

Read the full story

This is the first FBS college football game ever played at Wembley Stadium — a genuinely new kind of event for a venue that has hosted NFL regular-season games since 2007, but never a college matchup. The Big 12 built the Union Jack Classic as part of a broader push toward international expansion under commissioner Brett Yormark, with the explicit goal of making it an annual fixture rather than a one-off novelty.

Kickoff is confirmed for 5:00 PM local time — 9:00 AM back home in Arizona — broadcast live on FS1, with FOX's Big Noon Kickoff pregame show airing live from the stadium beforehand, giving ASU a national broadcast stage most college programs never get outside their own conference footprint. Reuters first reported the matchup in September 2025, meaning the family has been able to plan around a confirmed game date for almost a full year by the time kickoff actually happens.

Kansas is officially the designated "home team" for this neutral-site Big 12 conference game, a scheduling quirk that matters for things like which sideline gets which color and who technically hosts media obligations, even though neither team has any more claim to Wembley than the other. For ASU specifically, this is the program's first-ever game played in Europe — a genuine first in a history that already includes plenty of memorable road trips, but nothing quite like this one.

The name itself is worth a beat. In its current three-cross form, the Union Jack dates only to 1801, when the Kingdom of Great Britain formally united with Ireland — younger, in flag terms, than the country it represents. It layers three older crosses on top of one another: St George's red cross for England, St Andrew's white diagonal for Scotland, and St Patrick's red diagonal for Ireland, added last. St Andrew's cross isn't an abstraction on this trip — it's the same saint behind the town and the Old Course this family walked earlier in the week. A game called the Union Jack Classic, played under a flag built from three older unions, is a fitting name for a week that moved through the actual history that flag represents.

AA195 — Heathrow to Phoenix

Read the full story

American Airlines' Phoenix-London route is one of the longer scheduled nonstops the airline flies from the UK — nearly the same great-circle distance as the outbound leg, but scheduled longer in the timetable because westbound transatlantic flights fight a headwind the whole way across, the same jet stream that helps eastbound flights arrive early.

Flying westbound, the plane crosses roughly eight time zones over the course of the flight, and because you're chasing the sun rather than racing away from it, the day stretches rather than compresses — watches go back rather than forward on arrival, the one genuinely pleasant kind of jet lag. It's also, unusually, possible to land in Phoenix on the same calendar date you took off from London, a small scheduling trick of geography that never quite stops feeling strange.

American Airlines flies this specific city pair as one of a relatively small number of true "long thin" routes in its network — high-value, lower-frequency nonstops connecting a major international gateway to a large but non-hub American city, rather than funneling everyone through a bigger connection point like Dallas or Chicago. Routes like this only work at all because of the same twin-engine efficiency that made the outbound 777-200ER flight possible in the first place.
